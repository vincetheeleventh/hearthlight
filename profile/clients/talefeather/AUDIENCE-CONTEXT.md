# Talefeather — Audience Context (CLIENT layer, not engine)

**Scope:** this file describes **one client of Hearthlight**: Talefeather, the grief / living-legacy
service. It is not a Hearthlight law and it does not apply to projects outside Talefeather work.

Hearthlight is the engine — a filmmaking pipeline that turns a spoken story into illustrated
narrative media. Talefeather is a *product running on that engine*, aimed at families recording a
parent. A commercial spot, a comedy short, or a music piece could run the same pipeline and none of
what follows would apply to it.

**Load this only when** the project's brief declares `client: talefeather`, or Vince says the work is
Talefeather / remembrance / living-legacy. Otherwise skip it — a project with no grieving family
should not inherit a grieving audience. `client: none` is the normal case.

Everything below is **context for judgment, not machine-checked configuration.** Resist the urge to
turn any of it into a flag the pipeline validates; that has been tried and it added ceremony without
adding value.

Distilled from the Talefeather customer-language research (June 2026); this is the *why* behind
Talefeather's craft rules, not a marketing doc.

---

## 1. The two cohorts (and where the pilot sits)

**Living-legacy** — parent alive, often declining. Anticipatory, guilt-tinged, shareable, gift-occasion driven (Mother's/Father's Day, a milestone birthday, a new diagnosis). Price-sensitive, prone to "someday." Their words: *"while she still remembers," "before it's too late," "we're losing him a little at a time," "grieving someone who's still alive."*

**Grief / remembrance** — after loss. Urgent, compulsive, private (not posted), higher willingness to pay. Triggered by a found voicemail, an anniversary, the panic of a fading memory. Their words: *"I'm starting to forget what she sounded like," "it feels like a betrayal," "it's all I have left of him," "I play it over and over."*

**Where McConaughey sits:** neither. It is a **public celebrity interview** — same emotional shape (a son, a father, asking permission to become himself) but no grieving family commissioned it, and the rights are constrained (PRD §3, private use only). Its role is **emotional proof-of-concept and top-of-funnel content** — the "turn" video that makes a living-legacy viewer think of their *own* father. It demonstrates the craft; it is not a remembrance commission. Treat celebrity stories as the *trailer* for the product, never the product.

---

## 2. The single most important finding: the voice is the treasure

The most visceral, recurrent fear across every grief source is not forgetting facts — it's **forgetting the sound of a parent**. *"I can't remember her voice anymore — it feels like a betrayal."* The found voicemail becomes *"all I have left of him."* A real competitor review: *"my grandkids will be able to scan that QR code and see their pop-pop laugh."* The voice and face are the prize.

**Craft consequence for Talefeather work (guidance, not a flag):**
- The **interview VO is the hero asset**, never a generated substitute: a synthesized voice would be
  the exact betrayal the grief cohort fears. The real voice, remembering, IS the product. Whether a
  given project can honour that is a production decision made per project — it is not systematized.
- The illustration serves the voice, not the reverse. The picture is the memory; the voice is the person. (`hearthlight-storyboard` lip-sync policy already encodes this: figures don't mouth the VO — the older voice remembers, the young figure is the memory.)
- Bundle and preserve the original audio always, even when the deliverable is illustrated stills. Never let a family face the fading-memory nightmare.

---

## 3. The competitor's fatal flaw is our reason to exist: "it doesn't feel like THEM / like art"

Every incumbent fails on the same craft axis. StoryWorth: *"my finished product is ugly, formatting is random."* Remento (its own Trustpilot corpus): *"lacked emotional depth and personalization."* The universal complaint: *"it reads like a list of answers, not a story,"* *"looks like a third-grade reading book,"* *"every book ends up looking the same."*

**Pipeline consequence:** (note — the engine already encodes these as its own craft laws; this section
is the *commercial* argument for why they matter to Talefeather, not their only justification)
- **Consistency-as-product is the answer to "every book looks the same."** The locked Asset Bible, the no-drift policy, and the verbatim style block aren't perfectionism; they're the entire competitive wedge. A story that drifts into generic AI gloss commits the exact sin customers already punish competitors for.
- **Narrative architecture is the answer to "a list of answers, not a story."** The Story Arc → Beat Sheet → A/V Script chain exists so the output is an *architected film*, not a transcript with pictures. Counterpoint (image not echoing words) is what makes it feel authored.
- **"It doesn't feel like them"** is answered by the character sheet's *signature details that survive abstraction* and, in commission work, by the **family questions** — research that asks "Ford or Chevy? phone in the kitchen or the hallway?" The family are the world experts; the questions are how the artifact becomes *theirs* and not plausible-generic.

---

## 4. The emotional engine is guilt and the running clock — not warm nostalgia

The behavior-driving emotion is **anticipatory guilt and regret** ("I should have done this sooner"), weaponized by a visible closing window. YouGov (n=6,090): 47% regret not recording someone now gone; only 33% have recorded anyone. The most potent register is the *turn* — a story that seems to be about one family, then pivots to the viewer's own parent and the clock.

**Pipeline consequence — register, not plot:**
- Talefeather films should hold a register of **tenderness under a running clock**, not sentimentality. The McConaughey call works because it's a small, specific, charged moment (permission, fear→blessing), not a greatest-hits montage. Specificity is the feeling.
- This sharpens the Stage 2 **detonation beat**: the single charged moment the piece exists to deliver. Grief-aware storytelling earns its turn through restraint, then lands one blow. (Echoes the outline rule: withhold the payoff until the turn. The detonation beat itself is an *engine* concept — every genre has one; this is just Talefeather's flavor of it.)
- The grief cohort converts on emotion, privately. Content leads with living-legacy hooks (shareable), but lets the voice/clock carry the emotional payload. Opening on death suppresses reach; opening on a *voice* invites it.

---

## 5. What this means for the McConaughey pilot specifically
- It is the **proof-of-craft trailer**, demonstrating that an interview moment can become an illustrated film that "feels like him" — the thing every competitor fails at. Build it to be undeniable on craft, because its job is to make a viewer trust the process with their *own* parent.
- Keep the real interview audio as VO where resources allow — the pilot should model the product's core craft claim, not break it.
- Rights stay private (§3); celebrity stories are the trailer, families are the product.
- Success isn't "a nice McConaughey video." It's: *a stranger watches it and immediately thinks of calling their own father.* That's the turn the research says drives the whole category.
